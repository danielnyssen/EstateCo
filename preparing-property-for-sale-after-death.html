Preparing a property for sale after death

Clear, clean and prepare a deceased estate property for valuation, sale or settlement.

Preparing a deceased estate property for sale is about more than clearing furniture. The home needs to feel safe, open, clean and ready for agents, photographers, valuers and buyers.

Quick takeaway: A cleared, clean and well-presented property is easier to photograph, easier to inspect and easier for buyers to imagine themselves living in.

The order matters

Families often start with repairs or real estate photography too early. In most cases, the best sequence is: secure the property, identify important items, clear contents, deep clean, tidy gardens, complete minor repairs, then book photography and inspections.

  1. Secure: keys, locks, insurance, utilities and access.
  2. Sort: personal documents, sentimental items and valuables.
  3. Clear: remove furniture, rubbish, unwanted contents and storage items.
  4. Clean: kitchens, bathrooms, floors, windows, walls and odour sources.
  5. Repair: small maintenance items that distract buyers.
  6. Present: garden tidy, pressure washing and light styling if appropriate.

What buyers notice first

Buyers and agents form an impression quickly. Clutter, odour, overgrown gardens and dark rooms can make a property feel neglected even when the home itself has strong potential.

  • Front garden, entry path and porch.
  • Smell when the front door opens.
  • Light, curtains, blinds and window cleanliness.
  • Kitchen benches, cupboards and appliances.
  • Bathroom condition, grout, mould and ventilation.
  • Garage, sheds and storage areas.

Clearance before valuation or after?

If the property is heavily furnished or cluttered, clearing before valuation can help agents see the home clearly. In some cases, a preliminary agent visit is useful first so the family understands what level of preparation is worthwhile.

SituationBest approach
Lightly furnished homeAgent appraisal first, then targeted declutter and clean.
Long-held family homeSort sentimental items, clear bulk contents, then appraise.
Hoarded or neglected homeSafety assessment, staged clearance, deep clean, then appraisal.
Urgent settlement or auction timelineUse a combined clearance and property prep plan.

Small improvements that can lift presentation

Not every deceased estate needs a renovation. Often the best return comes from modest improvements that make the home feel cared for.

  • Remove all unwanted furniture and loose items.
  • Deep clean kitchens, bathrooms and floors.
  • Wash windows and open curtains for natural light.
  • Trim lawns, edges, hedges and remove green waste.
  • Patch obvious wall marks or remove hooks where practical.
  • Pressure wash driveways, paths or outdoor entertaining areas.
  • Remove broken items from sheds and garages.

When to consider painting or repairs

Painting can help when walls are heavily marked, rooms are dark or colours are very dated. Repairs are worthwhile when obvious defects create doubt for buyers, such as broken handles, loose fittings, damaged doors or unsafe steps. The goal is not perfection; it is to remove distractions that reduce confidence.

What to leave in the property

After clearance, it can be useful to leave manuals, warranties, remotes, keys and any documents the agent or buyer may need. Everything else should have a clear purpose. Empty rooms usually photograph better than rooms full of mismatched furniture.

Agent-friendly tip: Ask for before and after photos. They help interstate family members, executors and agents confirm the property is ready without needing repeated visits.

Preparing for photography

  • Clear benches, floors, wardrobes and visible storage spaces.
  • Remove bins, cleaning products, loose cords and personal items.
  • Turn on lights and open blinds.
  • Check front entry, driveway and garden beds.
  • Confirm the property smells fresh and feels easy to walk through.
