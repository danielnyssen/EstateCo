Executor estate clearance checklist

A practical guide for executors managing a property after a death.

Being named executor can feel overwhelming. This guide explains the property clearance side of the role in plain English, so you can protect important items, keep beneficiaries informed and move the home toward sale, settlement or handover.

Quick takeaway: Your job is not to personally do every task. Your job is to make sure the estate is managed carefully, records are kept, and decisions are made in the best interests of the estate.

What an executor needs to do first

Before a property is cleared, the executor should make sure the home is secure, insurance remains in place, and obvious valuables, documents and sentimental items are protected. In many cases, the practical work can begin before every legal process is complete, but decisions should be documented and family expectations should be clear.

  • Secure the property, collect keys and check windows, doors and alarms.
  • Locate the will, death certificate, property documents, insurance papers and financial records.
  • Photograph rooms before anything major is removed.
  • Create a simple list of items to keep, donate, sell, store or dispose of.
  • Communicate the process to beneficiaries before clearance begins.

What should not be thrown out too early

Estate properties often contain documents and small items that look ordinary but matter later. Search drawers, filing cabinets, bedside tables, wardrobes, garages and sheds before bulk removal begins.

  • Wills, property titles, bank statements, tax documents and superannuation records.
  • Passports, birth certificates, marriage certificates and military service records.
  • Jewellery, watches, coins, medals, stamps, artwork and collectables.
  • Family photographs, letters, recipe books and personal keepsakes.
  • Keys, remotes, manuals, warranties and appliance documentation.

How to reduce family conflict

Most estate disputes do not start with high-value items. They start when people feel excluded from decisions. A simple process helps: photograph rooms, invite family members to identify sentimental items, set a deadline, then proceed with an agreed clearance plan.

Practical tip: Create a shared photo album for beneficiaries. It gives everyone visibility and can reduce repeated site visits, especially when family members live interstate.

Choosing a clearance company as executor

Look for a company that understands estate work, not just rubbish removal. The provider should be comfortable working in sensitive homes, separating items for family review, providing written quotes and documenting the completed work.

A simple executor timeline

  1. Week 1: secure the home, gather documents, notify key parties and take initial photos.
  2. Weeks 1–3: identify keep/sell/donate/dispose categories and speak with beneficiaries.
  3. Weeks 2–6: complete clearance, cleaning, minor repairs and garden tidy if required.
  4. Weeks 4–10: prepare the property for appraisal, photography, sale, rental or settlement.
  5. Completion: retain invoices, photos and notes for estate records.
Important: This guide is general information only and is not legal advice. Executors should speak with their solicitor or estate professional about probate, disputes, tax and distribution obligations.
