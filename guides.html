What is deceased estate clearance?

Deceased estate clearance is the process of sorting, removing, and managing the physical contents of a property after someone has died. It is one of the most time-sensitive and emotionally demanding tasks an executor or family member will face — and one of the least understood.

Unlike a standard house clean-out, estate clearance involves legal obligations, emotional sensitivity, and significant decisions about items of financial and sentimental value. Done well, it protects the estate's value and gives the executor the documentation they need. Done poorly, it can create legal exposure, family conflict, and lost financial value.

When does estate clearance happen?

In most cases, clearance happens after probate is applied for but before the property lists for sale. The timing depends on several factors:

FactorWhat it means
Probate timingClearance can begin before probate is formally granted in most states — particularly for perishables and sentimental items. Your solicitor can advise what's permissible.
Property ownershipIf the property was jointly owned and passes by survivorship, it may not form part of the estate and clearance can begin sooner.
Holding costsEvery week empty costs the estate in rates, insurance, utilities, and potentially mortgage repayments — a real financial incentive to move efficiently.
UrgencyPerishables, pets, medication, and valuables should be addressed within days. Full clearance typically follows within 2–8 weeks.

Practical note: You do not need to wait for probate to be granted to begin sorting the property. Contact your estate solicitor to understand what actions are permitted in your state before probate is complete.

What to keep, sell, and donate

Before clearance begins, the executor — with input from beneficiaries — should identify items in each category:

CategoryWhat belongs here
KeepJewellery, photographs, personal documents, heirlooms specified in the will, and items bequeathed to specific beneficiaries.
SellFurniture, antiques, artworks, collectables, vehicles, electronics, and household goods with market value. All proceeds returned to the estate.
DonateClothing, books, kitchenware, and everyday items in good condition. St Vincent de Paul, the Salvos, and Lifeline accept estate donations. Receipts provided.
DisposeBroken goods, perishables, hazardous materials. Professional services handle responsible disposal including recycling and proper waste management.

Who is responsible for estate clearance?

The executor named in the will has legal responsibility for managing the estate, which includes organising clearance of the property. If there is no will (intestacy), an administrator is appointed — typically the next of kin — who takes on the same obligations.

The executor does not have to perform the physical work themselves. They can — and in most cases should — engage a professional estate clearance service to carry out the work on their behalf. The cost of clearance is a legitimate estate expense, payable from estate funds.

Typical clearance timeline

1
Days 1–7: Immediate actions

Secure the property, address perishables, identify urgent valuables (jewellery, cash, documents). Notify relevant parties of the death.

2
Weeks 1–3: Apply for probate

Lodge probate application with your state Supreme Court. Your solicitor manages this. Probate typically takes 4–8 weeks once lodged.

3
Weeks 2–6: Clearance and preparation

Professional clearance of contents, deep cleaning, minor repairs, garden tidying. Items sold, donated, or disposed of. Full documentation provided.

4
Weeks 4–10: Property to market

Agent engaged, property listed, inspections held, offer accepted. Clearance cost deducted from settlement proceeds.

5
Completion: Estate distribution

Estate accounts finalised. Beneficiaries receive their entitlements. Final estate report filed with probate records.

Not all clearance services are the same. Verify the following before engaging a provider:

What to checkWhy it matters

Workers compensationEnsures you have no liability if a worker is injured on the property.

Your role as executor

Being appointed executor is a significant legal responsibility. You are the person the law holds accountable for gathering the estate assets, paying debts, and distributing what remains to the beneficiaries. You do not need to do everything yourself — you can engage solicitors, accountants, and professional services — but you are responsible for ensuring it is done correctly.

Important: You are entitled to reimbursement for reasonable out-of-pocket expenses from the estate. In some cases, a court may approve executor commission for your time. Document everything.

Immediate actions (first 48–72 hours)

  • Obtain the death certificate from the hospital, doctor, or funeral director
  • Locate the will and confirm you are named as executor
  • Secure the property — change locks if necessary
  • Identify and secure cash, jewellery, and important documents
  • Notify the deceased's employer, bank (to freeze accounts), and Centrelink
  • Arrange for any pets to be cared for
  • Address perishable food and urgent medical matters
  • Notify immediate family and key contacts

Probate and legal checklist

  • Engage an estate solicitor to advise on probate requirements in your state
  • Locate and review the will — note any specific bequests
  • Commission a professional estate valuation of contents for probate purposes
  • Lodge a Grant of Probate application with the Supreme Court of your state
  • Register the death with Births, Deaths and Marriages in the relevant state
  • Notify the ATO of the death and lodge any outstanding tax returns
  • Contact the Land Titles Office regarding real property
  • Notify superannuation trustees — super does not automatically form part of the estate

Property checklist

  • Continue paying council rates, insurance, utilities, and mortgage during the estate period
  • Engage a professional estate clearance service — obtain a fixed written quote
  • Identify items for family members to collect before clearance begins
  • Arrange deep cleaning, minor repairs, and garden tidying post-clearance
  • Engage a real estate agent to assess and list the property
  • Obtain a digital estate report at clearance completion — required for executor records
  • Confirm the clearance cost can be paid at settlement from estate proceeds

Accounts and finances

  • Open a dedicated estate bank account to receive and hold estate funds
  • Cancel ongoing subscriptions, memberships, and direct debits
  • Notify all banks and financial institutions of the death
  • Arrange for mail redirection
  • Collect and document all estate assets: bank accounts, shares, superannuation, property, vehicles, valuables
  • Pay all outstanding debts from the estate — mortgage, credit cards, loans, utility arrears
  • Obtain probate-compliant invoices for all estate expenses (clearance, cleaning, legal fees)

Distributing the estate

  • Prepare a final estate accounting showing all assets, debts, and expenses
  • Distribute cash bequests first, then specific items, then the residual estate
  • Obtain receipts from all beneficiaries confirming they have received their entitlement
  • File the final executor's account with the Supreme Court if required
  • Retain all records for at least 7 years in case of future claims or challenges

Liability note: Distributing the estate before all debts are paid — including to the ATO — can make you personally liable as executor. Always pay debts first and confirm there are no outstanding claims before distributing.

Is estate clearance tax deductible?

Deceased estate clearance costs are generally treated as a legitimate estate expense — that is, deductible against the estate, not against the personal income of the executor or beneficiaries. This means the cost reduces the taxable value of the estate rather than creating a personal deduction.

The ATO treats estate administration expenses (including clearance, legal fees, and property preparation) as allowable deductions against estate income or capital gains. Your estate solicitor or accountant can advise on the specific treatment for your estate.

Important: Always obtain a GST-compliant, itemised invoice from your clearance provider. This is required for estate accounting and ATO compliance. EstateCo provides full probate-ready documentation as standard.

What is pay at settlement?

Many families face a practical problem: they need the property cleared before they have access to estate funds — but don't have cash available to pay upfront. Pay at settlement solves this.

With a pay-at-settlement arrangement, the cost of the clearance is deducted from the property's sale proceeds at settlement — meaning you pay nothing upfront, and the estate effectively funds the service itself. EstateCo offers this arrangement for eligible deceased estate clearances, subject to agreement with the executor or estate solicitor.

Who manages estates remotely?

More Australians are managing deceased estates from a distance than ever before. This includes children who have moved interstate for work, expat Australians living in the UK, USA, Singapore, New Zealand, or elsewhere, and family members scattered across different states. It also includes professional executors — solicitors and trustee companies — managing properties on behalf of their clients.

The good news: with the right service provider, you do not need to attend the property at all. Every step of the process — from initial quote to final documentation — can be managed digitally.

When you cannot be present, visibility is everything. A professional estate clearance service should provide:

What is probate?

Probate is the legal process by which a court formally recognises the validity of a deceased person's will and grants the executor the legal authority to deal with the estate. It is administered by the Supreme Court in each Australian state and territory.

In practical terms, a Grant of Probate is the document that allows the executor to access the deceased's bank accounts, sell or transfer real property, and distribute the estate to beneficiaries. Without it, most banks and the Land Titles Office will not release or transfer assets.

When do you need probate?

Not every estate requires formal probate. It is generally required when:

  • The deceased owned real property (land or a house) in their sole name
  • The deceased had bank accounts or investments above a certain threshold (typically $50,000–$100,000 depending on the institution)
  • Any financial institution or government body requires it as a condition of releasing assets

Probate is generally not required when all assets were held jointly (and pass by survivorship), or when the estate is very small. Your solicitor can advise based on the specific assets involved.

The probate process — step by step

1
Lodge Notice of Intention

File with the relevant state Supreme Court registry. Some states require a publication period before the application can proceed.

2
Gather required documents

Original will, death certificate, and an inventory of assets and liabilities. A professional estate valuation assists significantly here.

3
Lodge the probate application

Filed with the Supreme Court. Most Australian states accept electronic lodgement through their online portal.

4
Court review

The court examines the application and issues a Grant of Probate if satisfied. Typically 4–8 weeks from lodgement.

5
Administer the estate

With probate granted, the executor can collect assets, pay debts, clear the property, and distribute the estate.

Probate timing and estate clearance

A common misconception is that you must wait for probate to be granted before any work can be done on the property. This is not correct in most cases.

In all Australian states, the executor can take reasonable steps to preserve and manage the estate before probate is granted — including securing the property, identifying urgent items, and beginning clearance of perishables and personal effects. Full professional clearance typically can proceed once the executor has confirmed their role and obtained a copy of the will.

However, you should not sell or transfer real property until probate is granted. Your solicitor can provide specific guidance for your state.

When there is no will — intestacy

If the deceased died without a valid will, the estate is managed under the relevant state's intestacy legislation. A family member — usually the spouse, then children, then next of kin — applies to the Supreme Court for Letters of Administration rather than Probate. The process is broadly similar but may take longer.

Intestacy laws determine who inherits the estate according to a fixed statutory formula. This is why having a current, valid will is so important — intestacy can produce outcomes that do not reflect the deceased's actual wishes.
