Hoarded property cleanout guide

A respectful, safety-first approach to clearing cluttered or long-neglected homes.

Hoarded and severely cluttered properties need patience, safety planning and a non-judgemental approach. This guide explains how to clear a property while protecting people, valuables and the home itself.

Quick takeaway: Hoarded property clearance is not just “rubbish removal”. It often involves safety risks, emotional distress, hidden valuables, pests, mould, access issues and staged decision-making.

What makes hoarded property clearance different?

In a standard cleanout, contents are usually easy to identify and remove. In a hoarded or long-neglected property, important documents, money, jewellery, medication, family photos and valuables may be mixed with general waste. Rooms may also have blocked access, odour, mould, pests or trip hazards.

Start with safety and access

The first goal is safe movement through the property. Clear access paths to entry points, bathrooms, kitchens, bedrooms, electrical switchboards and exits. Avoid rushing into full removal until risks are understood.

  • Check for sharp objects, broken glass, needles or unsafe flooring.
  • Watch for mould, pest activity, animal waste or rotting food.
  • Identify blocked exits and areas where items may fall.
  • Use appropriate PPE if contamination is possible.
  • Keep utilities, electrical hazards and water damage in mind.

A respectful staged process

  1. Assessment: review access, hazards, volume and family priorities.
  2. Stabilise: create safe paths and remove obvious hazards.
  3. Search: identify documents, valuables, photos and sentimental items.
  4. Sort: separate keep, donate, recycle, dispose and specialist waste.
  5. Remove: clear contents in stages with appropriate disposal.
  6. Clean: deep clean, deodorise and prepare for repairs or sale.

What might be hidden in a hoarded home?

Item typeWhere it may be found
DocumentsDrawers, boxes, bags, filing cabinets, under beds and wardrobes.
Cash or jewelleryClothing pockets, tins, envelopes, bedside tables and kitchen cupboards.
Photos and keepsakesAlbums, boxes, suitcases, cupboards and old furniture.
Hazardous itemsSheds, garages, bathrooms, kitchens and laundries.
Valuable collectablesDisplay cabinets, storage boxes, garages and spare rooms.

Family communication matters

Hoarding situations can carry shame, grief and family tension. A respectful plan avoids blame and focuses on practical steps. Decide who can approve disposal, what must be kept, and whether photos of uncertain items should be sent before removal.

Important: If the person is still living in the home, clearance should be handled slowly and respectfully. Sudden removal without consent can be distressing and may not address the underlying issue.

Cleaning after clearance

Once contents are removed, the property may need deep cleaning. This can include floors, walls, kitchens, bathrooms, odour treatment, pest-related cleaning, mould attention and removal of residue. Some homes also need repairs before they are safe to occupy or ready for sale.

When specialist remediation is needed

  • Strong odour, mould or biological contamination.
  • Drug residue, chemical contamination or unknown substances.
  • Sharps, bodily fluids or animal waste.
  • Fire damage, water damage or structural safety concerns.
  • Pest infestation or unsanitary kitchen/bathroom conditions.

How to choose a hoarded property cleanout provider

Choose a provider that is discreet, insured and comfortable working in complex homes. They should be able to explain the process, provide a written scope, separate important items and coordinate cleaning or remediation where required.

Warning: Avoid providers who treat hoarded homes as simple rubbish jobs. The wrong approach can result in lost valuables, unsafe work and unnecessary distress for the family.
